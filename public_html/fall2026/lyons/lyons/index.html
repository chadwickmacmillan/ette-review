Arts and Craft

by Amy Lyons

My mother hand-stitched into the lining of my father’s old coat the money he’d budgeted to buy my birthday dress, hid the coat in the hall closet, then sat at her Singer, smiling conspiratorially. She whispered: stand still. As a bolt of cerulean satin flung outward from her hands, I was struck with the sensation that she’d pulled the whole suburban sky through the small window above her craft corner, gifting it to me. Draping the cool fabric across my shoulders, wrapping and tucking and pinning, tickling me sometimes to make her dangerous task seem fun, she sculpted the sky into a shape that looked like something they sold at the shop. Remember, she said, laughing so as not to scare me, what do we say if Daddy should ask? We linked pinkies. Mom bought it, I answered, sealing our swear. She kissed me, warm and strong, then lifted my arms, slid the skeleton of the dress off my six-year-old body, and started the machine.

Thrilled to behold the flamboyant seamstress buried beneath my mother’s 1950s housewife facade, a desire to take up my own tools roused me: I pulled out my sketch book to capture this strange, mother-shaped spirit. The hem she jackhammered into the slippery fabric, the hiss of the pedal she pumped with a bare foot she pedicured herself after thickening the coat’s lining with the salon allowance my father allotted, the long ash threatening to fall from her brown cigarette, all of it I recorded in charcoal, every line and curve a map of my mother’s secret self. Legs spread, she sung and sweated my dress to life. I’d never before seen her wear pants.

New York City, August, 1970, thousands of protesters swarm the hot streets. My mother and the two women we’d moved in with some months after my seventh birthday party, carry the long banner stitched with the collective’s demands: equal pay, reproductive rights, access. I walk with them, waving my sketch, which I’d hot-glued to a picket and updated with a wish that women like my mother would no longer be forced to line an old coat in exchange for their freedom: no fault divorce, now!

I feel the danger of what we’re doing out here when my five-year-old daughter yanks her hand from my fist and dashes away. You’re my baby, it’s dangerous, you could get yourself killed, I think, forcing myself not to say it out loud. Wanting her to savor the goodness of girlhood for as long as life allows, I shut my mouth and affix my watchful eyes to her quick body. She raises her face to feel the sun on her skin, chants along with the crowd like it’s Friday night at the park sing-a-long, points at the rows of pigeons lining wires. She wanders and skips. When she blithely pulls a stranger’s shirttails, my heart thuds with fear. You look very smart, I hear the old woman say. I am, my daughter answers, but grandma’s even smarter. Seeeeeee? My girl twirls and curtsies. She made my blue dress!

Amy Lyons is a two-time Pushcart nominee whose fiction has appeared in Waxwing, Prime Number, The Rose Books Reader, Best Microfictions and more. Former VP of the Los Angeles Drama Critics Circle, her nonfiction has appeared in LA Weekly, Backstage, The Independent, and Chapter 16. She holds an MFA from Bennington and is an alum of Vermont Studio Center, Millay Colony, and Tin House. Amy is the author of Seizures, a memoir about mother loss and the search for home. She lives in Nashville.
