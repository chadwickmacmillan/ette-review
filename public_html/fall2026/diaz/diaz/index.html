Headed to Venice

by Jose Hernandez Diaz

A man in a “Frida Kahlo for President” shirt drove on the 5 freeway to Venice Beach. He saw neon words painted onto the walls along the freeway: “Apart,” “Echo,” “Spanto.” It was getting close to Halloween. The night air was getting cool. The man in a “Frida Kahlo for President” shirt was driving to skate with a friend. He was getting too old for skateboarding but couldn’t abandon it yet. It still filled him with adrenaline and a sense of freedom and creativity. His daily profession was more technical, structured, as an editor for a national sports magazine. The Dodgers were headed back to the World Series. The city was regaining optimism after the illicit ICE raids of the summer. The sun was burning off the overcast smothering the city. He turned the radio louder: the new Kendrick Lamar album.

Rat Race

by Jose Hernandez Diaz

A man in a “The World Is Burning” shirt stood ready at the starting line of the race. He was in a rat race against the robot uprising. The robots were undeniably fast, strong and agile. The man in a “The World Is Burning” shirt woke up at 4 am to prepare for the rat race against the robot species. The robots did not need exercise or practice. The man in a “The World Is Burning” shirt headed to the final lap of the MC Escher-like rat race. A pair of robots sprinted slightly ahead of him. The man in a “The World Is Burning” shirt finished the rat race against the robots in third place. He celebrated by drinking a bottle of sweet champagne and declared the end of systematic hierarchy. From now on “races” would instead be celebrated as “festivals.” The robots willingly and humbly agreed. The following year, the festival of the “Rat Race” celebrated the fact that the world didn’t burn down after all.

***

Jose Hernandez Diaz is the author of The Lighthouse Tattoo, Bad Mexican, Bad American, Portrait of the Artist as a Brown Man, and the forthcoming Split/Mezcla: New and Selected Poems. A National Endowment for the Arts fellow, his work has appeared in Poetry, The Nation, and The Yale Review, among others. He has served as Poet in Residence at the University of Tennessee.
